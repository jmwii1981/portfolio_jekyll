<article class="vega-story-block" id="vega-story" tabindex="-1"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-overview-title">Bringing teams together through a shared design system</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>We kept reinventing the button across products. Accessibility was an area the company hadn’t been giving enough attention, and I used it to make the case for consistent, predictable design across our products. We started with the button, then moved into text size, the color palette, and other shared elements.</p>
<p>The company was already encouraging teams to work together, streamline processes, and give our products a shared visual identity. We had a direction, but we were still learning what a design system could be and where to begin.</p>
<p>I started by creating something we could work with together.</p>
</div></div></article>
<article class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-1-title">Making the idea tangible</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>I created the Paladin Wireframing Kit to show product leaders and stakeholders how reusable components could help us explore ideas quickly without getting caught up in visual details.</p>
<p>With engineers in the conversation, we could discuss feasibility as we sketched. They could explain technical constraints, and designers could respond with different proposals while everyone was still working through the idea.</p>
<p>That collaboration felt much more efficient than building a high-fidelity design, seeking approval, and repeating the process. It also gave designers room to introduce possibilities that might not otherwise have come up.</p>
<p>Alongside the kit, I worked with marketing and branding to understand the Heartland brand and bring them into the conversation. Across discussions and reviews, we explored how product design and marketing could share a visual direction.</p>
<p>The Paladin landing page was my mockup of that direction. The wireframing kit helped us explore how we could work together; the landing page helped us see what a shared identity could look like.</p>

<section class="vega-chapter" id="decisions" data-dock-section data-dock-label="Decisions" aria-labelledby="vega-decisions-title">
<article class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-decisions-title">Finding a foundation</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>When a new VP joined, I explained what I wanted to build. She gave me a small budget to purchase Pegasus, an existing design system kit we could use as a starting point for Vega.</p>
<p>An outside system gave us something to mold like clay, without choosing one team’s existing system over another’s.</p>
<p>We dissected the Pegasus Figma components to understand how they were built and how their variations worked. I worked with the team to develop components and variations around our requirements, including accessibility and the visual direction we had explored through Paladin.</p>
<p>I was also learning about the development side of design systems. I explored Stencil.js, Tailwind CSS, Storybook, and the Figma API, then brought those possibilities to the VP.</p>
<p>With her support, the effort grew. Dedicated designers worked on Figma components, an engineering group we called the Tiger Team began developing the technical side, and a UX writer later joined to help with documentation.</p>

<section class="vega-chapter" id="experience" data-dock-section data-dock-label="Experience" aria-labelledby="vega-experience-title">
<article class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-experience-title">Growing into product ownership</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>As more people joined, my role changed. I moved away from the detailed design and technical work and found myself leading from the center, helping the teams stay connected to the vision and to each other.</p>
<p>Informal discussions grew into whiteboarding sessions, standups, and a roadmap. I ran meetings, created milestones, organized our Notion workspace, and helped turn our discussions into stories the team could work from.</p>
<p>The initial idea had started with me, but the vision developed through collaboration. I facilitated workshops where we used affinity mapping to organize our thinking and reach decisions. We co-created user stories so that requirements reflected the people doing the work.</p>
<p>I also took company-sponsored product-owner training. After about a year of carrying those responsibilities, I received the title.</p>

<article class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-4-title">Making room for other people’s ideas</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>Some of Vega’s useful additions came from teammates showing me a possibility I hadn’t considered.</p>
<p>One lead introduced me to design tokens and explained how the Figma API could help connect changes in our master components to the codebase.</p>
<p>For the documentation website, I initially wanted to use WordPress. When IT security wouldn’t allow it, a teammate proposed Jekyll. They walked me through a demonstration, and I agreed to that direction.</p>
<p>Another lead suggested a dedicated Figma plugin. It helped designers create tables, access Vega’s icon library, use preconfigured shapes based on our grid, and bring in component variations that were difficult to work with in Figma at the time.</p>
<p>My role was to keep the purpose clear while giving people room to contribute their knowledge and shape the solution.</p>

<section class="vega-chapter" id="results" data-dock-section data-dock-label="Results" aria-labelledby="vega-results-title">
<article class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-results-title">Putting Vega into use</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>We released Vega Beta as a Figma component library so designers could begin using it while the broader system continued to develop.</p>
<p>The development library, plugin, and documentation followed. When the design and development libraries were available and comprehensively explained, we introduced Vega 1 at a company town hall.</p>
<p>People cheered. They were excited to use it.</p>
<p>Adoption still took work. Some engineering teams initially pushed back, and the Tiger Team made itself available for questions and support. When teams encountered roadblocks, I encouraged them to work together and draw on each other’s experience.</p>
<p>Meanwhile, my own product team was building a merchant payments platform with Vega from the ground up. Applying the system brought new needs into view. We needed more components and clearer rules of use as our work extended beyond the web into applications and different hardware.</p>
<p>During my time with Vega, I saw a product successfully launch with the system implemented. Around that point, I also saw other engineering teams begin adopting it.</p>

<article class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-6-title">What stayed with me</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>What I’m proudest of is that Vega brought teams together and helped create more consistent visual and functional experiences across our products.</p>
<p>It was also my first experience as a manager. Looking back, I would have spent more time strengthening individual relationships across teams. I was focused on keeping the work moving, and I could have given those relationships more attention.</p>
<p>Vega gave me experience creating a starting point, helping people develop it together, and carrying a shared vision into a working product. The people around me brought knowledge and ideas that made it better than I could have made it alone. I learned a lot from them, and their ideas continue to inspire how I work today.</p>
</div></div></article>
