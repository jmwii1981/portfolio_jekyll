<section aria-labelledby="vega-overview-title" class="vega-story-block" id="vega-story" tabindex="-1"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-overview-title">Starting with reusable components</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>The first step was to give people a way to explore ideas together without getting caught up in visual details. I created the Paladin Wireframing Kit so we could assemble workflows from reusable components rather than start each design from scratch.</p>
<p>We began with the button, then worked through text size, the color palette and other shared elements. The kit gave product leaders, stakeholders and the team something concrete to work with, helping us focus our conversations on how a product should work before deciding how it should look.</p>
<p>Working through concepts together let development begin while the design was still taking shape. We typically moved from whiteboard to code in a couple of weeks, compared with the month or two it had previously taken to get development started.</p>
</div></div></section>
<section aria-labelledby="vega-story-1-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-1-title">Working through design decisions together</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>I wanted technical constraints to be part of the design conversation before a high-fidelity design went through approval. I brought engineers into our early sketching discussions so they could explain those constraints and designers could propose alternatives. That let us work through feasibility together while the ideas were still young.</p>
<p>Marketing and product were working in separate silos. I created the Paladin landing-page mockup to give us something concrete to work on together and explore how the Heartland brand could carry through into our products. The mockup brought us into a shared conversation about the visual direction, with something we could review and develop together.</p>
</div></div></section>
</section>
<section class="vega-chapter" id="decisions" data-dock-section data-dock-label="Decisions" aria-labelledby="vega-decisions-title">
<section aria-labelledby="vega-decisions-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-decisions-title">Choosing a foundation</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>As we moved toward a shared system, we needed a foundation teams could shape together. Choosing one team’s existing system would have positioned its work as the standard for everyone else. I explained my approach to leadership and received support and a small budget to purchase Pegasus, an existing design system kit.</p>
<p>I chose an outside foundation so we could build something together without positioning one team’s work as the standard everyone else had to follow. That reduced resistance and gave teams a stronger sense of ownership, encouraging collaboration and adoption as we moved Vega forward.</p>
<p>We examined how the Figma components and their variations were built, then developed them around our requirements, including accessibility and the direction explored through Paladin. I researched development tooling and brought options to leadership as the work grew. Designers developed the Figma library, the engineering Tiger Team built the technical library, UX researchers joined to measure results, and a UX writer helped with documentation.</p>
</div></div></section>
</section>
<section class="vega-chapter" id="experience" data-dock-section data-dock-label="Experience" aria-labelledby="vega-experience-title">
<section aria-labelledby="vega-experience-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-experience-title">Taking on product ownership</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>As more people joined to shape Vega, my responsibility grew from creating the starting point to coordinating our shared work. I organized milestones and a roadmap, maintained our Notion workspace and helped the team turn discussions into user stories. Workshops and whiteboarding gave us a way to compare ideas and make decisions together, producing shared requirements and work the team could move forward.</p>
<p>I took company-sponsored product-owner training and received the title after about a year of carrying those responsibilities.</p>
</div></div></section>
<section aria-labelledby="vega-story-4-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-4-title">Decisions we made as a team</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>The team brought ideas that strengthened Vega. I worked with them to decide which would make the greatest difference for the teams using it and what we could take on within our budget and timeline.</p>
<p>One lead introduced me to design tokens and explained how the Figma API could connect changes in master components to the codebase. Another proposed a Figma plugin to address work that was difficult to manage in Figma at the time. The plugin helped designers create tables, access our icon library, use shapes configured to our grid and work with component variations.</p>
<p>For the documentation website, I initially preferred WordPress, but IT security ruled it out. A teammate proposed Jekyll and demonstrated it, and I chose that direction. We had an approach for the documentation website that respected the security constraint, informed by the team’s expertise.</p>
</div></div></section>
</section>
<section class="vega-chapter" id="results" data-dock-section data-dock-label="Results" aria-labelledby="vega-results-title">
<section aria-labelledby="vega-results-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-results-title">Releasing Vega and supporting adoption</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>The Figma library was ready before the rest of the system. We released it as Vega Beta so designers could begin using the components while we continued developing the technical library, plugin and documentation. Once the libraries and guidance were available, we introduced Vega 1 at a company town hall.</p>
<p>The response was enthusiastic, but some engineering teams initially pushed back on adoption. The Tiger Team made itself available for questions and support, and I encouraged teams to work through roadblocks together. Our shared work continued through adoption: making the libraries available was a starting point, and helping teams put them into practice came next.</p>
<p>My own product team was building a merchant payments platform with Vega from the ground up. Using the system in that work exposed gaps in the components and rules of use as we moved beyond the web into applications and different hardware. Those gaps gave us concrete needs to work through as the system developed.</p>
<p>What started with a button at Heartland grew into a multinational design system adopted by teams across Global Payments. Within my own portfolio, Vega shipped across eight products spanning payments, ACH and billing. Beyond those products, adoption extended to teams in the United States, Ireland, India and elsewhere in Asia.</p>
<p>That expansion brought new demands: different languages and reading directions, software platforms, hardware, interaction methods and accessibility needs. We were developing a shared foundation that had to accommodate those differences while keeping the customer experience consistent.</p>
</div></div></section>
<section aria-labelledby="vega-story-6-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-6-title">From one to together</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>Vega became more than a living product. It became a community. As we came together to shape it, we built new relationships and strengthened existing ones, connecting people across teams through shared work.</p>
<p>I learned that consistency depends on making room for those differences. We built Vega together, and through it, we became more connected in how we worked and what we created.</p>
<p>Many perspectives, one shared purpose—a community we built together.</p>
</div></div></section>
