<section aria-labelledby="vega-overview-title" class="vega-story-block" id="vega-story" tabindex="-1"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-overview-title">Starting with reusable components</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>Teams were rebuilding familiar components across products, and discussions about visual details could get in the way of exploring how a workflow should work. I built the Paladin Wireframing Kit to give us reusable components we could work with together, starting with the button and extending into text size, the color palette and other shared elements.</p>
<p>The kit gave product leaders, stakeholders and the team a way to explore workflows without having to settle every visual detail first.</p>

<section aria-labelledby="vega-story-1-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-1-title">Working through design decisions together</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>I wanted technical constraints to be part of the design conversation before a high-fidelity design went through approval. I brought engineers into our early sketching discussions so they could explain those constraints and designers could propose alternatives. That let us work through feasibility together while the ideas were still easy to change.</p>
<p>Marketing and product were working in separate silos. I created the Paladin landing-page mockup to give us something concrete to work on together and explore how the Heartland brand could carry through into our products. The mockup brought us into a shared conversation about the visual direction, with something we could review and develop together.</p>

<section aria-labelledby="vega-decisions-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-decisions-title">Choosing a foundation</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>Choosing one team’s existing system as the foundation would have positioned its work as the standard for everyone else. I explained my approach to leadership and received support and a small budget to purchase Pegasus, an existing design system kit.</p>
<p>I chose an outside foundation so we could build something together without positioning one team’s work as the standard everyone else had to follow. That reduced resistance and gave teams a stronger sense of ownership, encouraging collaboration and adoption as we moved Vega forward.</p>
<p>We examined how the Figma components and their variations were built, then developed them around our requirements, including accessibility and the direction explored through Paladin. I researched development tooling and brought options to leadership as the work grew. Designers developed the Figma library, the engineering Tiger Team built the technical library, and a UX writer joined to help with documentation.</p>

<section aria-labelledby="vega-experience-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-experience-title">Taking on product ownership</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>As more people joined, coordinating the work became a larger part of my role. I organized milestones and a roadmap, maintained our Notion workspace and helped the team turn discussions into user stories. Workshops and whiteboarding gave us a way to compare ideas and make decisions together, producing shared requirements and work the team could move forward.</p>
<p>I took company-sponsored product-owner training and received the title after about a year of carrying those responsibilities.</p>

<section aria-labelledby="vega-story-4-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-4-title">Decisions we made as a team</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>The team brought ideas that strengthened Vega. I worked with them to decide which would make the greatest difference for the teams using it and what we could take on within our budget and timeline.</p>
<p>One lead introduced me to design tokens and explained how the Figma API could connect changes in master components to the codebase. Another proposed a Figma plugin to address work that was difficult to manage in Figma at the time. The plugin helped designers create tables, access our icon library, use shapes configured to our grid and work with component variations.</p>
<p>For the documentation website, I initially preferred WordPress, but IT security ruled it out. A teammate proposed Jekyll and demonstrated it, and I chose that direction. We had an approach for the documentation website that respected the security constraint, informed by the team’s expertise.</p>

<section aria-labelledby="vega-results-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-results-title">Releasing Vega and supporting adoption</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>The Figma library was ready before the rest of the system. We released it as Vega Beta so designers could begin using the components while we continued developing the technical library, plugin and documentation. Once the libraries and guidance were available, we introduced Vega 1 at a company town hall.</p>
<p>The response was enthusiastic, but some engineering teams initially pushed back on adoption. The Tiger Team made itself available for questions and support, and I encouraged teams to work through roadblocks together. That support was part of putting Vega into practice, beyond making the libraries available.</p>
<p>My own product team was building a merchant payments platform with Vega from the ground up. Using the system in that work exposed gaps in the components and rules of use as we moved beyond the web into applications and different hardware. Those gaps gave us concrete needs to work through as the system developed.</p>
<p>Vega shipped in all eight products I led. Beyond those products, I saw other engineering teams begin adopting it. We had moved from separate teams rebuilding components to a shared system being used across products, creating more consistent visual and functional experiences.</p>

<section aria-labelledby="vega-story-6-title" class="vega-story-block"><div class="vega-editorial"><h2 id="vega-story-6-title">What I learned</h2><div class="vega-prose"><p>Vega was my first experience as a manager. Working with people whose expertise differed from mine taught me how to develop an idea with a team and make decisions about the work we could deliver together.</p>
<p>Looking back, I would have spent more time building individual relationships across teams. I was focused on keeping the work moving and could have given those relationships more attention. The people I worked with made Vega better than I could have made it alone. I learned a lot from them, and their ideas continue to inspire how I work today.</p>
